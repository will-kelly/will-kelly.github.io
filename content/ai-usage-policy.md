**I use AI. I don't outsource my thinking to it.**

*How I use artificial intelligence in my writing, research, content operations, and technical work.*

Artificial intelligence is part of my professional toolkit. I use it to research, challenge assumptions, organize ideas, develop software, build content pipelines, and occasionally get past the blank page.

What I don't do is confuse generating an answer with knowing what I'm talking about.

I've spent enough years working in technology to recognize the difference between a tool that improves the work and a tool that makes mediocre work easier to produce at scale.

AI can do both. The difference is usually the human operating it.

This policy explains how I use AI on willkelly.com and in the professional work I publish under my name.

## 1. My thinking comes first

My articles, arguments, opinions, and conclusions are my own.

AI may help me explore an argument, uncover a blind spot, test a counterargument, or organize a particularly nonlinear collection of ideas. It doesn't get to decide what I believe.

I bring my professional experience, skepticism, observations, and judgment to the work.

Sometimes AI helps me articulate an idea more clearly. Sometimes it challenges me. Sometimes it's spectacularly wrong.

Knowing the difference is my responsibility.

**I don't need AI to have an opinion. I use it to make sure that opinion survives scrutiny.**

## 2. AI assistance isn't a substitute for authorship

I use AI across different stages of writing and content production, including:

- Research discovery and identifying sources worth investigating.
- Brainstorming, outlining, and developing arguments.
- Challenging assumptions and exploring opposing viewpoints.
- Drafting and revising content where appropriate.
- Editing for clarity, structure, consistency, and readability.
- Developing metadata, summaries, and alternative formats.
- Building, testing, and documenting content production workflows.

That means some published material may contain AI-generated or AI-revised language.

I don't pretend otherwise.

But generating text isn't the same thing as exercising editorial judgment. I decide what gets published, what gets rewritten, and what belongs in the trash.

I won't claim that every word was written without AI assistance when that isn't true. I also won't pretend that using AI somehow eliminates the experience and judgment behind the work.

**Authorship carries accountability, regardless of which tools helped produce the words.**

## 3. Facts aren't optional

AI systems can produce convincing nonsense. Fluency isn't evidence, and a confident answer isn't a verified answer.

My standards are straightforward:

- Significant factual claims should be checked against credible sources.
- Statistics, quotations, and research findings must not be invented.
- Sources must support the claims attached to them.
- AI-generated citations aren't trustworthy until verified.
- Uncertainty should be acknowledged rather than disguised as authority.
- Opinion, analysis, speculation, and established facts should remain distinguishable.

I may use AI to find potential sources. That doesn't mean I've verified them merely because a chatbot provided a link.

When I get something wrong, I own the correction.

**A hallucination doesn't become a fact because it passed through an editorial workflow.**

## 4. I don't manufacture experience

I won't use AI to invent interviews, customer conversations, professional accomplishments, case studies, product testing, or firsthand experiences.

If I describe something as personal experience, it needs to be my experience.

If I publish a demonstration, simulation, or hypothetical scenario, I should identify it accordingly.

If I present software as working, I should have evidence that it works. A prototype isn't a production deployment. Generated code isn't tested code. A polished screenshot isn't proof of a functioning system.

This matters particularly in AI, where the distance between an impressive demonstration and an operationally sustainable solution can be enormous.

**I won't sell generated confidence as earned expertise.**

## 5. My voice isn't available for automation

I have a recognizable perspective on technology, work, enterprise software, and the sometimes ridiculous rituals surrounding corporate transformation.

That perspective comes from years of observing what organizations say, what they do, and what happens when the two collide.

AI can help me express that perspective. It shouldn't flatten it into generic thought leadership.

I reject the idea that professional writing must sound like a quarterly earnings call, a management consultant's slide deck, or a motivational poster with a technology budget.

If AI produces language that doesn't sound like me, it gets rewritten or discarded.

My voice, including its occasional contrarian streak, remains mine.

## 6. Privacy and intellectual property matter

I don't treat public AI tools as unrestricted dumping grounds for confidential information.

My operating principles are:

- Don't submit confidential client information without appropriate authorization and safeguards.
- Don't expose personal, employee, customer, or proprietary information unnecessarily.
- Respect contractual obligations, licensing requirements, and intellectual property rights.
- Review the data-handling practices of tools used in professional workflows.
- Apply appropriate access controls and human review to AI integrations.
- Don't assume a vendor's privacy claims substitute for organizational governance.

AI convenience doesn't override confidentiality.

A faster workflow isn't worth much if it creates a data exposure problem someone else has to clean up.

## 7. Automation must earn its place

I'm interested in operationalizing AI, not collecting AI subscriptions or building agents for the sake of having agents.

When I design or evaluate an AI-enabled workflow, I care about whether it solves a real problem.

That means asking:

- What problem are we solving, and for whom?
- Why does this need AI rather than a simpler solution?
- What systems and information does it depend on?
- Where does human judgment enter the process?
- How are quality, accuracy, and failures measured?
- Who owns the output and the consequences?
- Can someone maintain, audit, and improve the workflow after the demonstration ends?

An agent without meaningful boundaries, oversight, or accountability isn't an AI strategy.

It's an operational liability waiting for a sufficiently creative failure.

**The objective isn't maximum automation. It's better outcomes with defensible controls.**

## 8. Disclosure should be meaningful

I don't believe every use of spelling assistance, transcription, outlining, or editing software requires a ceremonial AI confession.

I do believe readers and clients deserve transparency when AI materially affects the work or when its use changes how they should evaluate a result.

My approach is proportional.

Routine assistance may not receive a separate disclosure on every page. Substantial AI-generated content, synthetic media, simulations, or other uses that materially affect interpretation should be identified when relevant.

For client work, contractual disclosure requirements and agreed AI usage restrictions take precedence.

Disclosure isn't a substitute for quality control, and disclosure alone doesn't make questionable work acceptable.

## 9. I reserve the right to disagree with the machine

I use AI as a collaborator, research assistant, development tool, and sometimes an unusually persistent debate partner.

I don't consider it an authority.

I expect to challenge its recommendations, reject its conclusions, inspect its output, and occasionally decide that the better answer is to do the work without it.

The same skepticism applies to AI vendors, consultants, executives, and anyone promising that a new model will somehow eliminate the organizational problems they haven't bothered to understand.

Critical thinking doesn't become obsolete when software gets more capable.

It becomes more important.

## 10. The responsibility stops with me

If something appears on this website under my name, I'm responsible for the editorial decisions behind it.

Not OpenAI. Not Anthropic. Not Google. Not whichever model or tool happened to participate in the workflow.

Me.

That doesn't mean every publication will be flawless. It means errors should be corrected, claims should withstand scrutiny, and technology shouldn't become an excuse for avoiding responsibility.

I expect the same accountability from organizations deploying AI at scale.

## The bottom line

I believe AI can make experienced professionals more effective. It can help people explore ideas, build useful systems, remove repetitive work, and uncover possibilities that might otherwise remain buried.

I also believe it can industrialize bad assumptions, accelerate sloppy decisions, and create an extraordinary volume of content nobody needed.

I've worked in technology long enough to know that a tool's capabilities and an organization's ability to use them responsibly are two very different things.

My approach is to use AI aggressively where it adds value, skeptically where it makes claims, and carefully where people, privacy, or professional credibility are at stake.

**I use AI to extend my capabilities, not impersonate capabilities I don't have.**

That's my policy.

And unlike a surprising number of corporate AI policies, I intend to follow it.
