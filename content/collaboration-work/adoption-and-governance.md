---
title: "Adoption and governance"
description: "How collaboration and AI systems become sustainable ways of working through ownership, behavior, decision rights, and maintenance."
---


Deployment is an event.

Adoption is a change in behavior.

Governance is the operating structure that keeps the new behavior from turning into another round of platform sprawl.

Those three ideas often get bundled together in transformation programs, then measured through the easiest available signals: licenses assigned, training completed, pages migrated, active users, or AI prompts submitted.

Those measures can describe activity. They do not tell you whether the organization created a sustainable way of working.

**A collaboration system becomes real when people know where work belongs, which behavior is expected, who owns the rules, and what happens when the system stops matching reality.**

## Define the operating change before the rollout

Teams often start a collaboration project with the platform.

They choose features, configure spaces, migrate content, build templates, and plan training. The operating decision arrives later.

That reverses the problem.

Before rollout, the organization should be able to explain what is changing in the work itself.

For example:

- where a project begins
- where durable knowledge belongs
- how decisions are recorded
- which system owns tasks
- who maintains templates and taxonomy
- what belongs in chat versus a durable source
- how exceptions are handled
- what teams may customize
- what must remain consistent

Without those decisions, implementation teams end up using configuration to compensate for missing governance.

The product becomes the policy.

## Make governance useful to the person doing the work

Governance fails when it exists only as a document about governance.

People need rules they can use while making decisions.

That means governance should show up in the structure of the system:

- named owners
- clear destinations
- permission models that match responsibility
- templates that encode important decisions
- review paths
- visible source status
- escalation routes
- lifecycle triggers
- observability where automation or AI acts

The strongest governance often feels less like a gate and more like a well-designed road.

People can tell where to go.

## Treat local variation as a design problem

Central teams often want consistency. Individual teams want freedom.

Both positions contain something useful.

Too little structure creates fragmentation. Too much structure creates shadow systems because teams rebuild what they need outside the official platform.

The practical problem is deciding which parts of the operating model need to be shared and which can remain local.

Shared elements might include:

- identity and access
- source-of-truth rules
- security boundaries
- organization-wide taxonomy
- required decision records
- archival responsibilities
- integration standards
- AI access policies

Local elements might include:

- team views
- lightweight workflow stages
- working templates
- project-specific metadata
- local automation
- naming conventions inside a bounded space

The exact split depends on the work.

The important thing is to make the split intentional.

## Measure friction and workarounds

A system can show high usage while people hate the way it works.

They may use the platform because they have no choice, then compensate with spreadsheets, private notes, side channels, duplicate trackers, or manual copy-and-paste rituals.

Those workarounds are valuable evidence.

They show where the official operating model does not fit the work.

Instead of treating every workaround as noncompliance, ask what problem it solves.

- Is the official workflow too slow?
- Is information hard to find?
- Are permissions wrong?
- Does the platform lack a useful view?
- Is a required field meaningless?
- Does a governance rule conflict with how the team actually delivers?
- Are users protecting themselves from unreliable data?

Adoption research should look for the gap between declared process and lived process.

That is where design work begins.

## Give ownership a lifecycle

Many collaboration systems launch with clear ownership and decay after the implementation team moves on.

Administrators leave. Business owners change roles. A center of excellence loses funding. Taxonomy decisions stop being revisited. Integrations break quietly. Templates stay frozen while the work changes around them.

Governance needs its own maintenance model.

Someone should own questions such as:

- Which rules still matter?
- Which platform assumptions changed?
- Which teams are creating duplicate structures?
- Which content or workflows are abandoned?
- Which integrations or agents have excessive access?
- Which controls generate work without reducing risk?
- Which user behaviors signal that the system no longer fits?

A governed system is not a static system.

It is a system with a way to change deliberately.

## Treat AI adoption as organizational change

AI adds another layer because it changes both capability and behavior.

Employees may use approved assistants, consumer tools, embedded copilots, internal agents, or workflows other teams built. The formal AI program can therefore be smaller than the actual AI environment.

Governance needs to account for where AI enters the work, what information it can reach, and what actions it can take.

Useful questions include:

- Which tasks are appropriate for experimentation?
- Which workflows need stronger review?
- Which sources may the system use?
- What data should remain outside the workflow?
- Who owns an internal agent after its creator changes roles?
- How are users told when AI influenced an output or action?
- How do corrections change the underlying system?

Training matters. So do permissions, source quality, process ownership, and incentives.

AI adoption cannot be delegated to prompt literacy.

## Use governance to reduce ambiguity

Good governance should make fewer decisions mysterious.

It should reduce the number of moments where users have to ask:

"Where does this go?"

"Which version is right?"

"Who approves this?"

"Can the agent do that?"

"Who owns this now?"

"What happens if I change it?"

When those answers are built into the operating system, governance becomes part of usable work instead of a compliance appendix.

That is the standard I use when looking at collaboration platforms, knowledge systems, and AI-enabled workflows.

## Research questions

This section will examine questions such as:

- Which adoption signals reveal meaningful workflow change?
- How much standardization does a collaboration platform need before users route around it?
- What governance can be encoded in the product rather than documented separately?
- How should organizations detect abandoned ownership?
- Which workarounds signal healthy local adaptation versus systemic failure?
- How do AI agents change permission and accountability models?
- What should a collaboration center of excellence own after initial rollout?
- How should teams retire obsolete rules, spaces, automations, and agents?

The long-term test is not whether the platform launched.

It is whether the organization can keep using it without accumulating a new layer of invisible operating debt.
