**Start with the operating problem.**

I work at the intersection of technical knowledge, content, AI, and go-to-market teams. My job is to help good ideas survive the handoff between strategy and execution.

That means getting close to the people doing the work, understanding the systems they already use, and finding the missing connections between expertise, ownership, and delivery.

## Make the handoff work

The operating layer is where plans meet actual constraints. My approach centers on three things:

### 1. Knowledge you can trust

Clear sources. Defensible claims. Useful context.

Before improving a workflow, I want to know where the information comes from, who can verify it, and what it is safe to use. Better writing, better AI outputs, and better decisions all depend on trustworthy source material.

### 2. Ownership that is clear

Named decisions. Review that has a purpose.

Every handoff needs an accountable person, especially when technical accuracy, customer commitments, or editorial judgment are involved. I make decision rights, review responsibilities, and escalation paths visible rather than assuming a tool will resolve them.

### 3. Workflows people can use

Build it. Test it in the work. Keep it useful.

I prefer small, meaningful implementations over elaborate plans. Start with a real task, test with the people responsible for it, inspect what fails, and improve what earns its place. The outcome should be understandable and maintainable without constant intervention from its creator.

## Where I usually begin

I look for the broken handoff, undocumented process, missing context, duplicated effort, or impressive demo that does not fit actual work. The problem may sit between a writer and subject-matter expert, between sales and product marketing, or between a knowledge base and an AI-assisted workflow.

I work closely with engineers, solution architects, writers, product marketers, and knowledge owners. Their expertise should shape the solution, not be treated as a late-stage approval requirement.

## What the work looks like

1. **Understand the existing system.** Identify the task, people, sources, decisions, constraints, and baseline.
2. **Find the consequential gap.** Separate the real operating problem from a symptom or a fashionable technology proposal.
3. **Design the smallest useful intervention.** Define what changes, who owns it, how it fits existing tools, and what success would look like.
4. **Build and validate in context.** Test against real use cases, source integrity, handoffs, human review, and failure conditions.
5. **Document and transfer ownership.** Leave the team with a repeatable practice, not a dependency on a presentation or a particular person.
6. **Measure and adjust.** Evaluate quality, effort, reuse, adoption, and maintainability before deciding whether to expand.

## The through-line

Writing led me to the systems behind the work: sources, ownership, review, knowledge flow, and the handoffs between teams. I kept moving upstream because that is where recurring problems began.

I use AI when it helps, but I do not outsource my understanding. I want to be able to explain the reasoning, limits, and consequences of the systems I recommend.

**Strategy becomes repeatable execution when people have the knowledge, responsibility, and workflow to make it work.**
