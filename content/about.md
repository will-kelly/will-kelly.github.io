I'm a writer who learned to think in systems.

Over the course of my career, I've worked across technical writing, B2B technology content, content strategy, and the operational work that makes all three possible. I've spent a lot of time translating complicated technology into something people can understand and use, particularly in cloud, DevOps, security, and enterprise software.

But the work that interests me most often starts where the job description stops: the broken handoff, the undocumented process, the missing context, or the tool that looks impressive in a demo but doesn't fit how people actually work.

Today, I describe my focus as **content and AI workflow strategy**. I bring an editorial background, technical curiosity, and a systems mindset to the problem of making AI useful inside real organizations.

## From writing about technology to making it work

Writing has never been just about producing words for me. Good technical and B2B content requires understanding the product, the audience, the surrounding systems, and the business problem. It also requires knowing when a claim doesn't hold up.

That perspective pushed my work beyond individual deliverables into content operations, documentation practices, knowledge management, editorial standards, and repeatable workflows. I became increasingly interested in what happens before a draft reaches the writer and after it leaves the editor: where the source material lives, how decisions get made, how quality is measured, and who owns the process when something breaks.

I still care about clear writing. I care just as much about the system that allows people to produce and maintain it without starting from scratch every time.

## Why I'm focused on operationalizing AI

Generative AI can accelerate useful work. It can also accelerate confusion, publish weak assumptions more efficiently, and hide gaps in organizational knowledge behind polished output.

That's why my interest isn't in adding AI to everything. It's in understanding where AI belongs in an existing workflow, what information it can trust, where human judgment remains essential, and how to tell whether the result is actually better.

I work on the practical questions:

- **Integration:** How does an AI-enabled workflow connect to the tools, documents, repositories, and approval steps people already use?
- **Governance:** Where do claims, editorial standards, access controls, and accountability live?
- **Adoption:** Can people use the workflow without depending on its creator to explain every step?
- **Maintenance:** What happens when the source material changes, a model behaves differently, or a tool disappears?
- **Measurement:** What evidence would show that the system improved quality, turnaround, consistency, or access to knowledge?

A convincing demonstration is a start, not an operating model.

## Building, testing, and documenting the ideas

My current hands-on work explores AI-assisted content pipelines, editorial quality controls, docs-as-code, and enterprise knowledge workflows.

Those explorations include **Pipeline Lab**, an evolving concept and implementation effort for testing content workflows from intake through review; **DocOps** experiments built around Python, Markdown, and GitHub; and frameworks for content consistency, reusable claims, and human editorial oversight.

I use tools such as Hugo, GitHub, Python, Notion, and custom GPT workflows when they fit the problem. I don't regard a long tool list as a strategy. A smaller system that is understandable, maintainable, and genuinely useful is usually the better outcome.

Some of these projects are prototypes or works in progress, not production deployments. I make that distinction because working code, a successful demo, and a sustainable operational system are three different things.

## The way I think

I'm a dyslexic, systems-oriented thinker. I tend to see connections, dependencies, and alternate routes before I see a neat sequence of steps. That can make me the person asking why a process exists, what assumption everyone is accepting, or what happens three steps downstream.

It also explains a recurring pattern in my career: **I tend to own the gaps.** Not because every gap needs another committee or another platform, but because overlooked connections are often where a project succeeds or fails.

Some of my best professional experiences have been working alongside engineers, solution architects, and technically curious teams who value questions, independence, and evidence. I work best where critical thinking is welcome and expertise matters more than organizational theater.

## What I write about

My writing examines the intersection of technology, work, and the people expected to make both function. I question AI hype, empty change-management rituals, content production for its own sake, and the idea that buying a tool is equivalent to solving a problem.

I also write about career experience, institutional knowledge, and why effective teams need a mix of perspectives rather than assumptions about which generation understands technology best.

I use AI extensively in my own workflows, but **I don't outsource my understanding**. If I publish an argument or recommend a system, I need to be able to explain its reasoning, limits, and consequences myself.

## Where I'm headed

I'm interested in roles and collaborations that connect **AI enablement, enterprise knowledge, content operations, and technical strategy**, especially where the mandate is to turn fragmented work into a coherent, accountable system.

I can lead with the narrative, map the workflow, work with the technology, and challenge the assumptions that hold a project back. I don't expect one person to be the expert in everything. I do expect the people building a system to understand what they're building and why.

If you're working on a problem where content, knowledge, technology, and execution keep getting separated, that's a conversation worth having.
